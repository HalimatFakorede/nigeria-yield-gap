# Policy Brief, Nigeria's Growth Model in Food Production Is Running Out of Land

**Halimat H. Fakorede** | Analysis of FAOSTAT and World Bank data, 1961-2024 | 8 staple crops

---

## Summary

Nigeria's food-crop output has grown substantially since 1990. Almost none of that growth came from productivity. **128% of the growth in eight staple crops came from expanding cultivated area; the yield contribution was −28%, meaning average yields fell.** Food output per person peaked in 2006 and is 10.5% below that peak today.

This matters because the two growth paths have different ceilings. Area expansion is finite and competes directly with forest, grazing land and settlement. Yield growth is not. A strategy built on the first has an end date.

---

## Four findings

**1. The growth has been extensive, not intensive.**
Cassava area grew 334% while cassava yields fell 44%. Yam area grew 385% while yields fell 25%. Rice area grew 196% with yields flat. In each case Nigeria produced more by farming more land, less well.

**2. Maize is the counter-example, and it matters.**
Maize yields rose 84% on an almost unchanged area (+14%). Intensification is demonstrably achievable in Nigerian conditions. The question is why it happened for maize and not for roots and tubers, improved varieties and a commercial offtake market are the obvious hypotheses, and both are policy-actionable.

**3. Input intensity is the binding constraint, but not the whole answer.**
Nigeria applies **10.9 kg of nutrient per hectare**. Ghana applies 36.1, Ethiopia 37.8, Kenya 33.2; the world average is around 120. A panel fixed-effects regression across 221 countries and 11,615 country-years gives a yield elasticity of **0.087** (p < 0.001).

But re-estimated on the low-input half of the sample, where Nigeria sits, **the elasticity falls to 0.048**. Returns to fertilizer are *lower*, not higher, in low-input systems. This is consistent with the agronomic literature: response is capped by soil organic carbon, seed quality, moisture and application timing. **Fertilizer distribution alone will underperform its business case.**

**4. The gap is concentrated in roots, tubers and rice.**
Against rainfed African peers (minimum 100,000 ha harvested; Egypt and South Africa excluded as irrigated systems), Nigeria trails badly on cassava (−75% vs Ghana), yam and rice, but is at or above the peer median for maize, sorghum, cowpea and groundnut. Effort should concentrate where the gap actually is.

---

## Implications

| For | Implication |
|---|---|
| **Policy** | Measure agricultural programmes on yield per hectare, not output. Output targets can be met by clearing land, which is the outcome to avoid. |
| **Input programmes** | Bundle fertilizer with improved seed, soil testing and extension. The low-input elasticity result says unbundled fertilizer underdelivers. |
| **Investment** | Cassava and yam carry the largest absolute yield gaps and the weakest productivity trends, the biggest upside, and the clearest evidence of neglect. |
| **Climate** | Area-led growth is emissions-led growth. Any credible agricultural NDC has to shift the growth source to yield. |
| **Research** | Understand the maize exception. It is the only domestic proof that intensification works here. |

---

## Scale of the prize

Reaching the rainfed peer **median** yield on existing harvested area would add roughly **125 million tonnes** of output per year, concentrated in cassava, yam and rice.

*Caveat, stated plainly:* this is an order-of-magnitude figure, not a forecast. It holds area constant, uses imputed producer prices where FAOSTAT has no Nigerian series, and ignores the fact that supply increases of that scale would themselves move prices.

## What would sharpen this

National averages cannot identify *which* farmers are behind or why. The natural extension is World Bank LSMS-ISA plot-level panel data for Nigeria, which would allow yield response to be estimated at farm level with household characteristics, and would identify the specific segments where extension has the highest return.
